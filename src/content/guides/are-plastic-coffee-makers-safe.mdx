Are plastic coffee makers safe? US regulators allow the plastics in them: FDA says BPA "is safe at the current levels occurring in foods," and polypropylene is cleared for food contact under 21 CFR 177.1520. The EU disagrees on BPA and adopted a ban in December 2024. Coffee studies I found reported levels below the limits they compared against, but none tested a home drip brewer.

None of the numbers on this page are ours. They come from the agencies and studies linked in each section, and I've given the date for every agency position.

## What plastic touches your coffee in a drip machine

Water and coffee in an electric drip brewer pass through several parts. [iFixit's community troubleshooting page](https://www.ifixit.com/Wiki/Cuisinart_DDC-3200_Troubleshooting) for the [Cuisinart DCC-3200](/reviews/cuisinart-dcc-3200/), for example, names the water reservoir, the water hose under the base, the filter basket and the carafe lid. Which of these are plastic, and which plastic, varies by model, and the sources on this page don't list it for most machines.

Moccamaster is the clearest documented example I found. On its EU blog (November 12, 2025), the brand says the plastics in its coffee machines are free from BPA, BPF and phthalates, and that all parts that touch water or coffee are tested and comply with "the strictest European standards." That is a brand-wide manufacturer claim. The page doesn't name the plastic type, the standard or the testing body.

## What the FDA says about BPA in food contact

The FDA's position, on a page marked current as of April 20, 2023, is that "BPA is safe at the current levels occurring in foods." Its [BPA food-contact page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-packaging-other-substances-come-contact-food-information-consumers/bisphenol-bpa-use-food-contact-application) says the available information continues to support the safety of BPA for currently approved uses in food containers and packaging.

The FDA has removed a few uses from its rules. In July 2012 it stopped providing for BPA-based polycarbonate resins in baby bottles and sippy cups, and in July 2013 for BPA-based epoxy coatings in infant formula packaging. FDA says both changes were made because industry had abandoned those uses, not on safety grounds.

The review that page describes covered more than 300 studies published or available between November 1, 2009 and July 23, 2013. FDA completed it in fall 2014 and found no information that prompted a revision of its safety assessment. The page does not mention EFSA's 2023 reassessment.

## Why Europe reached a different conclusion on BPA

On April 19, 2023, the [European Food Safety Authority](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/bisphenol-food-health-risk) set a tolerable daily intake (TDI) for BPA of 0.2 ng per kg of body weight per day. That is about 20,000 times lower than its previous temporary level of 4 µg per kg. EFSA concluded that consumers with average and high exposure in all age groups exceed the new TDI. The effect it highlighted was on the immune system: an increase in a type of white blood cell, T helper cells, in the spleen.

EFSA also noted a limit of its own: it used exposure estimates from its 2015 assessment, and EU restrictions brought in after 2015 may have reduced exposure.

On 19 December 2024, the [European Commission adopted a ban](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety-news/commission-adopts-ban-bisphenol-food-contact-materials-2024-12-19_en) on BPA in food-contact materials, citing EFSA's assessment. Its examples are metal can coatings, reusable plastic drink bottles, water coolers and other kitchenware. Most products get an 18-month phase-out, with very limited exceptions, and other bisphenols harmful to the reproductive and endocrine systems are covered too.

The legal text is [Regulation (EU) 2024/3190](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R3190), published December 31, 2024 and in force 20 days later (January 20, 2025). Kitchen appliances fall under repeat-use articles. Those made with BPA may be first placed on the market until July 20, 2026 (January 20, 2028 for professional food equipment). They may stay on the market until January 20, 2029.

So FDA and EFSA disagree, and neither source I read addresses the other's conclusion directly.

## Do plastic coffee makers leach chemicals into coffee?

Some brewed coffee contains measurable phthalates and bisphenols, but every study I found reported levels below the limits it compared against. The table puts the two regulators beside every brewed-coffee measurement I found. Each source's own conclusion is in the last column.

Velotto's paper gives two different upper bounds for the professional-machine DEHP range: 21.32 ng/g in its Table 2 and 11.32 ng/g in its abstract. The median, 6.65 ng/g, is the same in both. The authors say the higher DEHP from professional machines could depend on their materials, such as seals and pipes, and how worn they are. Higher pressure may also play a part: 10 bar versus 1.5 bar in a moka pot. They also list not recording machine wear as a limitation.

All Velotto values were below the EU specific migration limits the authors cite: 1.5 mg/kg for DEHP and 0.3 mg/kg for DBP. Their highest estimate, six cups a day from a professional machine, came to 17.17 ng/kg bw/day of DEHP against a TDI of 50 µg/kg bw/day.

Sakaki's finding cuts both ways. BPA and BPF turned up in French press coffee, and the two positive French press brands were bought pre-ground rather than as whole beans. My reading is that the coffee itself can carry bisphenols, not only plastic machines, though the authors don't test that.

The drip-bag number is about single-serve pour-through bags, not machines, and rayon made up more than 80% of the particles released. None of these studies tested a household drip brewer.

## Does "BPA-free" mean a coffee maker is safe?

Not on its own. A BPA-free coffee maker label is a manufacturer claim, and none of the sources here describe a test or certification behind it. Moccamaster's blog goes further and also claims BPF- and phthalate-free, but names no testing body.

Substitutes matter. Sakaki et al. detected BPF, a BPA substitute per Moccamaster's own blog, in capsule and French press coffee. The EU ban covers other bisphenols harmful to the reproductive and endocrine systems, not just BPA.

Polypropylene isn't a bisphenol plastic: [21 CFR 177.1520](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-177/subpart-B/section-177.1520) defines it as polymers made from propylene. That section says olefin polymers, including polypropylene and polyethylene, "may be safely used" in articles that contact food, subject to its specifications, which include extractable-fraction limits.

## How to cut plastic contact in your brew path

None of the sources show a health benefit from these steps. They follow from the studies above, which is my reasoning, not a finding.

- **Consider a manual brewer with a glass, ceramic or steel path.** Sakaki's French press results show this doesn't guarantee zero bisphenols.
- **Replace worn hoses and seals.** Velotto's authors suggested wear in professional espresso machine parts could raise DEHP, though they didn't measure wear. Separately, iFixit's DCC-3200 page says that in many cases the water hose at the bottom of that machine is damaged or worn out after a year or two from overheating.

## Where the evidence on plastic coffee makers is thin

- No independent study I found measured migration from a home drip machine's reservoir, tubing or basket.
- FDA and EFSA disagree on BPA, and the US and EU now regulate it differently.
- The microplastic study measured particle counts, not health effects.
- The studies are small. Velotto analyzed 40 beverage samples, and Sakaki's positives came from a handful of samples per chemical.
- Velotto's own paper reports two different ranges for its highest-DEHP group.
