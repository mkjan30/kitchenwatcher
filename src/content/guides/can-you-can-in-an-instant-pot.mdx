Can you can in an Instant Pot? Not for pressure canning: the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP), hosted by the University of Georgia, says USDA canning processes are "not recommended for use in electric pressure multi-cookers at this time," even on models with a canning button or manuals that cite USDA. Water bath canning has no tested multicooker process either. Use a stovetop pressure canner that holds at least four quart jars for low-acid food, or a boiling-water canner for acid foods.

This page is research, not a kitchen trial: every claim below links to or names the agency, manual or publication it comes from, as described in [how we research](/how-we-research/). It covers preserving food in sealed jars only, with no process times or recipes. For those, use the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning.

## Is multicooker canning safe? Why NCHFP and USDA say no

NCHFP has said no three times: in a [2014 blog post](https://nchfp.uga.edu/blog/can-i-can-in-a-multi-cooker) (editor's note Feb 12, 2015), a [September 2015 statement on small pressure cookers](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-pressure-cookers), and a [February 1, 2019 statement on electric multi-cookers](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers). Its reasons:

- **No jar-level testing.** No USDA thermal-process work has tracked jar temperatures inside any electric pressure cooker, and jar position and steam flow affect those temperatures ([NCHFP, 2019](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers)).
- **Temperature matters, not pressure.** Reaching canning pressure doesn't prove the food heated evenly, and air left in the steam lowers the temperature at the same pressure ([NCHFP, 2019](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers)).
- **Come-up and cool-down count.** USDA process times rely partly on heat held while the canner cools, so the canner must cool naturally. A small cooker heats and cools faster and can under-process food ([NCHFP, 2015](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-pressure-cookers)).
- **Power fluctuations.** NCHFP asks whether power surges or drops could let the temperature fall too low, and how a user would know ([NCHFP, 2014](https://nchfp.uga.edu/blog/can-i-can-in-a-multi-cooker)).
- **Altitude.** The pressure needed changes with elevation ([NCHFP, 2019](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers)).
- **Canner size.** A USDA pressure canner must hold at least four quart jars upright on a rack, and USDA research used canners of 16 quarts or larger ([NCHFP, 2015](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-pressure-cookers)).
- **The risk is botulism** in under-processed low-acid food ([NCHFP, 2014](https://nchfp.uga.edu/blog/can-i-can-in-a-multi-cooker)).

None of this is new. Per NCHFP's 2015 statement, USDA advised against canning in small pressure cookers in the late 1980s, and a once-proposed fix of adding 10 minutes to the process didn't hold up as a general rule. NCHFP also says it can't convert its processes to other equipment.

## What does the Instant Pot canning setting on the Max say?

The Instant Pot Max has a Canning button. Its user manual (Copyright 2018 Instant Brands) describes a Canning Smart Program with two settings, Low at 6.5 psi for high-acid foods such as fruit, jams and chutney, and Max at 15 psi for "low-acid foods and meats" (manufacturer spec), with a cook time of 1 minute to 8 hours. It says the cooker holds up to 4 x 16 oz (500 mL) jars, warns "Do not pressure can at altitudes above 2000m (6600ft)," and tells users to follow the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning. These are Instant's claims, not tested processes.

The conflict is plain. The manual links to the USDA guide at an nchfp.uga.edu address, the site of the center whose 2019 statement says not to use multi-cookers, even when the manufacturer's directions include pressure canning. Four jars of 16 oz is below USDA's minimum of four quart jars. And the manual's pressure-canning steps have you select a venting method so the cooker releases pressure automatically when time is up, while NCHFP's process times count on the canner cooling naturally to 0 pounds.

Instant said it had outside work under way. Per [Food in Jars](https://foodinjars.com/blog/canning-in-an-instant-pot-max/) (Nov 26, 2018, updated Aug 24, 2022), Instant said a three-phase McGill University validation was under way, and that phase one found Low held about 110 to 111 °C and Max about 116.5 to 119 °C. That is a manufacturer claim relayed second-hand, and Food in Jars advised against buying the Max for canning until all phases were done. A web search on Nov 13, 2026 turned up no published result from the later phases.

Newer models don't carry the button. The Instant Pot Rio Plus Wide user manual lists a Sterilize program (10 minutes default, adjustable 0 to 30, default pressure level High) and no Canning program, and its text never mentions canning. Nothing in that manual presents Sterilize as a way to preserve food in jars.

## What independent measurements of electric pressure cookers found

The only independent measurements found come from Utah State University Extension. In a [May 10, 2019 news release](https://extension.usu.edu/news_sections/home_family_and_food/electric-pressure-cooker-and-canning), USU called the research preliminary and said electric pressure cookers did not always reach or hold the temperatures needed for safe low-acid canning at Utah altitudes. Project lead Cathy Merrill said, in short, that they don't get hot enough at those elevations. USU recommends traditional stovetop pressure canners.

The [USU research poster](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/files/foodsafetyelectricpressurecookers.pdf) gives the design. USU logged temperatures inside jars of chicken, green beans and pinto beans in three cookers (a Duo80 Instant Pot, a Power Pressure Cooker XL and a Carey Smart Canner and Cooker) at 2,917, 4,500 and 7,070 feet, against a 121.1 °C target. The Instant Pot and the Power Pressure Cooker XL never reached 121.1 °C. The Carey reached it in most conditions, depending on altitude and food, and USU said it still needs more research.

Altitude made a significant difference in every brand. Note the model: USU tested the Duo80, not the Max with its Canning program, and nothing below 2,917 feet.

[Reviewed](https://www.reviewed.com/ovens/features/is-this-new-instant-pot-feature-dangerous) (Apr 12, 2018, updated Aug 2020) made the related point that a 15 psi reading doesn't confirm food temperature, and that as far as its writers knew the Max had no internal thermometer. The evidence is thin: one university study at altitude, one manufacturer's unpublished phase-one claim, and no independent validation of any multicooker for canning.

## Can you water bath can in an Instant Pot?

The evidence says it hasn't been tested. NCHFP's statements cover USDA processes in multi-cookers generally, and the only electric exceptions it names are dedicated canners: the Ball Automatic Home Canner and the non-pressurized Jarden and Weck electric boiling-water canners ([NCHFP, 2019](https://nchfp.uga.edu/newsflash/canning-in-electric-multi-cookers)).

A USDA water bath process needs at least one inch of briskly boiling water over the jar tops, plus added time above 1,000 ft: 5, 10, 15 or 20 minutes depending on the altitude band, per [USU Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/research/principles-of-boiling-water-canning). No source found shows a multicooker holding a rolling boil at that depth over jars; the Max manual's own pressure-canning steps fill water only a quarter of the way up the jars, about 6 cups. So the honest answer is that nobody has shown it works.

[Reviewed](https://www.reviewed.com/ovens/features/is-this-new-instant-pot-feature-dangerous) wrote in 2018 that you can still use an Instant Pot for water bath canning, as long as you follow the appropriate guidelines and use acidic foods. That is an editorial view, not a tested process, and this page gives no how-to.

## What to use instead of an Instant Pot for canning

- **Low-acid food** (vegetables, beans, meat, poultry, fish, soups, per [USU](https://extension.usu.edu/news_sections/home_family_and_food/electric-pressure-cooker-and-canning)): a stovetop pressure canner that holds at least four quart jars, per NCHFP and [NDSU FN2064](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/pressure-can-it-right-tips-safely-using-stovetop-or-electric-pressure-canner).
- **Acid foods** with pH 4.6 or lower (fruit, pickles, sauerkraut, jams and jellies, and most tomatoes with added acid, per [USU](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/research/principles-of-boiling-water-canning)): a boiling-water canner.
- **Dedicated electric canners.** NCHFP's multicooker statement doesn't apply to the Ball Automatic Home Canner, which is for acid foods only. NDSU's FN2064 (revised Aug 2026) describes the Presto Precise Digital Pressure Canner as sensing temperature rather than pressure, but doesn't say USDA or NCHFP tested it. I'm not endorsing either.
