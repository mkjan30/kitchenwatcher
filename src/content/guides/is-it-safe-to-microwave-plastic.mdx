Is it safe to microwave plastic? Plastic labeled microwave safe is allowed: it passed FDA-reviewed tests that cap how much of its chemicals can migrate into food. The cap is not zero. A 2023 University of Nebraska-Lincoln study measured, in some cases, billions of nanoplastic particles per square centimetre from microwaved polypropylene containers. The FDA allows labeled plastic; America's Test Kitchen says skip plastic entirely.

The concern here is chemicals and particles from heated plastic. Leakage from the oven is a separate question, covered in [whether microwave radiation is safe](/guides/is-microwave-radiation-safe/).

## What does "microwave safe" mean on plastic?

Per [Harvard Health Publishing](https://www.harvardhealth.org/healthy-aging-and-longevity/microwaving-food-in-plastic-dangerous-or-not), manufacturers must test a container with FDA-specified methods, and the FDA reviews the data before a container can carry the microwave-safe icon or wording. The tests account for likely temperatures, the ratio of plastic surface to food, heating time and how often the food is eaten. The allowed migration is 100 to 1,000 times less, per pound of body weight, than the amount that harmed lab animals over a lifetime.

Harvard also says an unlabeled container is not necessarily unsafe. The FDA simply hasn't evaluated it.

[America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/7872-why-you-shouldnt-microwave-plastic) reads the label more narrowly: it means the container won't melt, explode or be damaged, and it is not a health claim.

The oven manuals agree on the basics. The [Toshiba EM131A5C-BS instruction booklet](https://images.homedepot-static.com/catalog/pdfImages/fb/fb6df202-147c-4227-8107-017db04f8ed2.pdf) says plastic should be "microwave-safe only," labeled as such, used per the maker's instructions, and warns that some plastic containers soften as the food inside gets hot. IKEA's GÅTEBO manual says the same. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens) adds that some plastic containers can be melted by the heat of the food inside.

> **Microwave safe, in one line:** the container passed FDA-reviewed tests for use at microwave temperatures. Chemical migration stays under FDA limits (Harvard Health), and per ATK the container won't melt or be damaged. It does not mean zero chemicals or particles reach the food.

## What the 2023 Nebraska microplastics study found

The study behind the "billions of particles" headlines was published in *Environmental Science & Technology* and led by Kazi Albab Hussain, per the [University of Nebraska-Lincoln release of July 21, 2023](http://news.unl.edu/article/nebraska-study-finds-billions-of-nanoplastics-released-when-microwaving). We read the university's release, not the paper itself.

The test conditions, per the release:

- Two polypropylene baby-food containers and one polyethylene reusable pouch, all FDA-approved plastics.
- Filled with deionized water or 3% acetic acid, which stands in for dairy, fruit, vegetables and other mildly acidic foods.
- Heated at full power for three minutes in a 1,000 W microwave.

In some cases, microwaving released more than 2 billion nanoplastics and 4 million microplastics per square centimetre of container. Experiments simulating six months of refrigerated or room-temperature storage also suggested particle release, so heat may not be the only route. The team's model estimated that infants drinking microwaved water and toddlers eating microwaved dairy take in the highest relative amounts.

When cultured embryonic kidney cells were exposed to the highest concentrations, only 23% survived after two days. Those doses stood for what infants and toddlers might accumulate over days or from several sources, not what one container releases. The release adds two caveats: the health effects of eating micro- and nanoplastics in people remain unclear, and kidney cells may be more sensitive than cell types studied before.

Resin-code lists, including [Healthline's](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/can-you-microwave-plastic), point readers to BPA-free #5 polypropylene as the alternative. This study tested polypropylene. Calling it the safe plastic doesn't square with a study that measured particles coming off it.

## BPA, phthalates and the dioxin myth

Harvard Health explains that BPA makes plastic clear and hard, and phthalates make it soft and flexible. Both are believed to be endocrine disrupters that mimic hormones. Both may leak into food microwaved in plastic, and fatty foods such as meat and cheese may absorb more. Harvard calls the dioxin warning a myth. Plastics don't contain dioxins, which form when things burn, so you aren't exposed as long as you don't burn your food.

Healthline says the FDA prohibits BPA in baby bottles, sippy cups and infant-formula packaging. The [FDA's own BPA page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-packaging-other-substances-come-contact-food-information-consumers/bisphenol-bpa-use-food-contact-application) is narrower. It stopped authorizing those uses because industry had abandoned them, a decision it says was not based on safety. Its current view is that BPA is safe at the levels now found in food. Healthline also says plastics sold as BPA-free can release BPS, BPF or phthalates when microwaved.

The evidence here is thinner than headlines suggest. [Food Network](https://www.foodnetwork.com/healthyeats/healthy-tips/2019/02/what-to-know-about-using-plastic-in-the-microwave) notes that the health studies behind these warnings were not about microwaving specifically.

## Can you microwave takeout containers?

America's Test Kitchen's [takeout guide](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/8073-which-takeout-containers-can-you-microwave) (March 14, 2024) says no to all of these:

- **Plastic deli containers:** heat speeds up leaching.
- **Styrofoam:** it can melt, and ATK notes styrene is "reasonably anticipated to be a carcinogen."
- **Foil trays:** they can arc; see [foil takeout trays](/guides/can-you-put-aluminum-foil-in-the-microwave/).
- **Chinese takeout boxes:** metal fasteners and plastic-coated board.
- **Compostable containers:** often bioplastic or plastic-coated fibre.

ATK also cites a [Consumer Reports test](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/food-contaminants/the-plastic-chemicals-hiding-in-your-food-a7358224781/) (January 2024) of 85 foods: bisphenols turned up in 79% of samples and phthalates in all but one food. CR says phthalate levels did not depend on packaging type, that the chemicals also enter food from processing equipment, and that none of the foods exceeded US or European limits for the few chemicals that have one.

Harvard Health says most takeout containers are not microwave safe, and trays sold as microwavable takeout dinners are for one use only. The sources split on foam: Harvard says some polystyrene is microwave safe if labeled, while ATK says no and the Toshiba and IKEA manuals say plastic foam may melt or contaminate the liquid inside.

## Plastic wrap: what the microwave manuals say

The Toshiba EM131A5C-BS booklet and IKEA's GÅTEBO manual both allow microwave-safe wrap only, and say not to let it touch the food. IKEA adds that wrap should be vented so steam can escape. Breville's BMO650 instruction book says to vent lids and wrap by turning back one edge to form a narrow vent.

Harvard Health suggests skipping wrap: wax paper, parchment, white paper towels or a domed cover over the plate do the same job. Toshiba and IKEA also say to pierce plastic pouches and slit boiling bags before heating.

## If you do use plastic, how to lower the risk

1. Use only containers labeled microwave safe, and follow the container maker's directions (Toshiba, Breville).
2. Throw out scratched, cracked or old containers; Harvard Health says worn ones may leach more, and Healthline adds that leaching rises with age and repeated dishwashing.
3. Vent the lid by leaving it ajar or lifting one edge (Harvard).
4. Don't microwave storage bags, grocery bags, tubs or water bottles (Harvard).
5. Watch the oven when heating food in plastic or paper, because of the risk of ignition (Breville). If something does catch, see [what to do in a microwave fire](/guides/microwave-fire-what-to-do/).
6. Unsure whether a dish is microwave safe? The Toshiba booklet's test is 1 cup of cold water next to the empty dish at full power for 1 minute; if the dish is warm, don't use it. That checks whether the dish heats up, not whether anything leaches.
7. The lowest-plastic option is glass or ceramic (ATK, Harvard), covered with wax paper, parchment or a white paper towel (Harvard).

For eggs, paper bags, superheated water and the rest, see [everything else to keep out of the microwave](/guides/what-not-to-put-in-a-microwave/).

## Our take: where the sources disagree

The FDA and the oven makers allow labeled plastic, and Harvard Health explains the safety margin behind the label. America's Test Kitchen says not to microwave any plastic. The Nebraska researchers measured large particle releases from FDA-approved plastics but say the effects on people remain unclear. The figures on this page come from the university's release; the journal paper itself wouldn't open for us.

My read: labeled plastic is within the rules, and the evidence of harm in people is thin. If you'd rather not bet on it, putting food in glass or ceramic first costs one extra dish. That is the most cautious choice, especially for infant and toddler food, the group the Nebraska model flagged.
