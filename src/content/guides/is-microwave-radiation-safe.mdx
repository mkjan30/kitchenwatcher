Is microwave radiation safe? Yes, for an oven whose door, hinges, latch and seals are undamaged. Per the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens), federal law caps leakage over an oven's lifetime at 5 mW/cm² about 2 inches from its surface, "far below the level known to harm people." About 20 inches away the reading is roughly 1/100th of that. The FDA says there is little cause for concern unless the door hinges, latch or seals are damaged.

## How a microwave keeps the radiation inside

[Panasonic's support explainer](https://help.na.panasonic.com/answers/how-to-test-for-microwave-radiation-leakage/) says its oven cavity is a Faraday cage, and the perforations in the door mesh are much smaller than the microwaves' wavelength, so the radiation cannot pass through the door or the vent holes. The FDA adds that microwaves are reflected by metal, which keeps them bouncing inside the cavity until food absorbs them.

The door is also wired to switch the magnetron off. [21 CFR 1030.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/1030.10), the federal performance standard, requires at least two operative safety interlocks. At least one must not be operable by any part of the body or by an object with a straight insertable length of 10 cm, and the failure of any single part may not disable all of them. One or both required interlocks are monitored, and if they fail the oven must become inoperable and stay that way until repaired.

Microwaves are non-ionizing, the FDA says, and they do not make food radioactive.

## The legal leakage limits, in numbers

Two limits apply in the US: a strict one when the oven is sold, and a looser one for the rest of its life. The FDA's "about 2 inches" and the regulation's "5 cm" are the same distance. Canada sets the same two numbers but ties them to whether a test load is in the oven (water, per Health Canada), per the [CCOHS fact sheet](https://ccohs.ca/oshanswers/phys_agents/microwave_ovens.html).

## How much do real ovens leak?

The legal limits matter less than what ovens actually emit. The only measured dataset I found is a survey by researchers at [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radiation/radiation-leakage-before-sale-used-microwave-ovens-health-canada-2000.html)'s Radiation Protection Bureau, published by the International Microwave Power Institute in 2000. The before-sale ovens were surveyed in spring 1997; the used ovens afterwards. That is thin, old evidence, and today's ovens may differ in either direction.

Sixty before-sale ovens from 14 makers averaged 0.3 mW/cm² with no load, against a no-load limit of 5. None exceeded the leakage limit, though six models from three makers were incorrectly labeled. The 103 used ovens from 17 makers ranged from under a year to 23 years old, about 8.5 on average. They averaged 0.52 with no load.

One used oven, about 1% of the sample, failed. It was 23 years old and measured 4.5 mW/cm² with a water load, over the 1 mW/cm² limit for that test. A 22-year-old oven stayed well within the limit, so the authors concluded age is not the primary factor. They list the most frequent causes of leakage as mechanical abuse of doors and seals, dirt around the door seals, improper servicing, and worn hinges and latches.

## Is it safe to stand in front of a microwave?

Yes, if the oven is in good condition. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens) gives narrower advice than either "it's a myth" or "keep away": don't stand directly against an oven for long periods while it's running, and don't let children do so.

Distance does most of the work. Per the FDA, a reading taken about 20 inches from the oven is roughly 1/100th of the reading at about 2 inches. Being in the same room is not what the FDA warns about.

Two other pages I read handle this loosely. Tasting Table calls the worry "little more than a modern-day wives' tale," then says the FDA recommends not standing in front of microwaves and advises keeping your distance, without reconciling the two. The FDA's actual wording is narrower: directly against the oven, for long periods. National Geographic quotes a physicist saying harmful malfunctions are exceedingly rare, but gives no limit or numbers.

## Microwave door damaged? When to stop using it

21 CFR 1030.10 requires a warning label on US ovens saying not to operate it with an object caught in the door, a door that doesn't close properly, or a damaged door, hinge, latch or sealing surface. The FDA says the label can be dropped only if the maker proves the oven stays within the limit even under those conditions. The [Toshiba EM131A5C-BS instruction booklet](https://images.homedepot-static.com/catalog/pdfImages/fb/fb6df202-147c-4227-8107-017db04f8ed2.pdf) and the [Breville BMO650 instruction book](https://assets.breville.com/BMO650/BMO650_USCM_IB_L24_LR.pdf) print matching precautions, and Breville's adds checks for a misaligned door and dents. Stop using the oven if any of these is true:

1. Something is caught between the door and the front face, such as a towel or food.
2. The door doesn't close firmly or latch properly.
3. The door is bent, warped, dented or misaligned.
4. A hinge or latch is broken or loose.
5. The door seals or sealing surfaces are damaged.
6. Soil or cleaner residue has built up on the sealing surfaces. Clean them before the next use; the cleaning steps are in [how to clean a microwave](/guides/how-to-clean-a-microwave/).
7. The oven runs with the door open. The FDA says to stop using it.

Breville's manual says that if the door or door seals are damaged, the oven must not be operated: "Immediately cease use and call Breville Consumer Support." Toshiba's booklet says not to defeat or tamper with the safety interlocks, and that only properly qualified service personnel should adjust or repair the oven. CCOHS adds: do not disable or bypass any safety locks.

Don't open the cabinet or try a fix yourself. Call the manufacturer or a qualified service technician. An oven that starts by itself is covered in [Toshiba microwave turning on by itself](/guides/toshiba-microwave-turning-on-by-itself/).

## Can you test a microwave for leaks at home?

Not reliably. The viral test of putting a phone inside and calling it proves nothing. Panasonic says "using the cell phone testing is not a proper way" to detect leakage, because phones work at about 800, 900 or 1,800 MHz while oven shielding is designed for 2,450 MHz. Leakage has to be measured with properly calibrated equipment that detects 2,450 MHz.

CCOHS also says special equipment is needed. Health Canada says that if you suspect excessive leakage, have the oven professionally inspected by the manufacturer, distributor or a service person.

Consumer leak detectors are sold, and Tasting Table suggests one. No source I read evaluates their accuracy, so I can't recommend one. That evidence is missing.

## Can microwave radiation harm you? Pacemakers, eyes and burns

Per the FDA, pacemaker interference from microwave ovens has "largely been resolved" by modern pacemaker shielding, and it advises asking your doctor if you're concerned. CCOHS, quoting Health Canada, says most pacemakers today are not affected by being near an oven whose leakage is within limits, and that anyone with a pacemaker who gets dizzy or uncomfortable near one should move away immediately and consult a health care professional.

High exposure can cause skin burns and cataracts, the FDA says. The eyes and testes are particularly vulnerable because they have relatively little blood flow to carry heat away. Those injuries take large amounts of microwave radiation. The FDA says there have been very rare radiation injuries due to unusual circumstances or improper servicing, generally from large amounts leaking through openings such as gaps in the seals.

The FDA says most microwave-related injuries are heat burns from hot containers, overheated foods or exploding liquids. Superheated water and exploding eggs are covered in [what not to put in a microwave](/guides/what-not-to-put-in-a-microwave/).

## If you suspect a leak: who to contact

1. If the door, hinges, latch or seals are damaged, or it runs with the door open, stop using it (FDA). Contact the manufacturer: the FDA says you may contact the maker about a suspected radiation safety problem, and Health Canada says to have the manufacturer, distributor or a service person inspect the oven professionally.
2. Report it to the FDA. Its microwave page says you can report suspected radiation problems or injuries by completing and mailing the Accidental Radiation Occurrence Report form, and lists DICE@fda.hhs.gov for consumer questions about microwave radiation. The FDA's general line is 1-888-INFO-FDA (1-888-463-6332).
3. Check whether your model has a CPSC recall in the [microwave recall check](/guides/microwave-recall-check/).
