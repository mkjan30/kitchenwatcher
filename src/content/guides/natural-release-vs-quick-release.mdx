Natural release vs quick release comes down to whether you want cooking to stop now or slowly. Quick release means moving the valve or switch to Vent so steam leaves right away; the [Rio Plus Wide manual](https://www.qvc.com/footers/cd/pdf/K89432_Manual.pdf) says to use it for foods that overcook, like vegetables and seafood. Natural release means leaving it on Seal; Instant's FAQ recommends that for high-liquid or starchy foods such as oatmeal, beans, porridge and soups. Either way, open the lid only after the float valve drops.

**Key takeaways**

- Natural release for oatmeal, beans, porridge and soups; no quick release for stews, chilis, pasta, congee, beans or grains (Instant FAQ).
- Quick release for foods that overcook, like vegetables and seafood (Rio Plus Wide manual).
- Breville's Fast Slow Pro adds a third mode, Auto Pulse, that releases steam in bursts (BPR700 manual).

## What is the difference between natural and quick release?

Instant's FAQ describes a quick release as switching the pot from sealing to venting, which lets the steam out at once. During a natural release, it says, "cooking stops gradually": the temperature drops and the pot depressurizes on its own while the valve stays on sealing.

The Rio Plus Wide manual gives the two procedures:

1. **Natural:** after cooking, leave the steam release switch at Seal until all pressure is released. The steam icon disappears, the display shows OK to Open Lid, and you remove the lid.
2. **Quick:** after cooking, move the switch to Vent and wait for all pressure to release. Again, wait for the steam icon to disappear and OK to Open Lid before removing the lid.

The practical difference is doneness. Instant's FAQ says cooking stops gradually during a natural release, so a recipe timed for one method can come out under- or overdone with the other. TwoSleevers, a recipe site, puts it this way: natural release time "counts as cooking time".

## When to use natural release vs quick release, food by food

Every row below comes from a manufacturer source. Instant's lists cover Instant pots; Breville's are the factory defaults for its Fast Slow Pro presets, from the Australian-edition BPR700 manual.

| Food or recipe type | Release method | What the source says | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oatmeal, porridge | Natural | Recommended for foods with large liquid volume or high starch | Instant FAQ |
| Soups | Natural | Listed with oatmeal, beans and porridge | Instant FAQ |
| Beans and grains | Natural; no quick release | Foods that expand when cooked; don't quick release | Instant FAQ |
| Stews, chilis, pasta, congee | No quick release | Fatty, oily, thick or high-starch foods | Instant FAQ |
| Vegetables | Quick | Foods that tend to overcook | Rio Plus Wide manual; Instant FAQ |
| Seafood | Quick | Foods that tend to overcook | Rio Plus Wide manual |
| Stock, Legumes, Pot Roast, Pudding presets | Natural (preset default) | Keeps stocks and beans from churning or breaking apart | Breville BPR700 manual |
| Rice, Potatoes, Custom presets | Auto Pulse (preset default) | Bursts that reduce disturbance to the food | Breville BPR700 manual |
| Risotto, Soup, Casserole, Lamb Shanks, Bolognese (incl. beef chili) presets | Auto Quick (preset default) | Minimizes overcooking of short-cook recipes | Breville BPR700 manual |

The reason starchy and foamy foods get natural release is splatter. Instant's FAQ says quick release "can cause splattering" with high-liquid and high-starch foods. The Rio Plus Wide manual lists applesauce, cranberries, pearl barley, oatmeal and other cereals, split peas, noodles, macaroni, rhubarb and spaghetti as foods that can foam, froth, sputter and clog the steam vent. For those foods, it says to fill no higher than the 1/2 line. Fill lines are covered in [how much water to put in a pressure cooker](/guides/how-much-water-in-pressure-cooker/).

### Where the manuals and recipe sites disagree

The sources don't agree on soup, stew and chili. Instant's FAQ says natural release for soups and no quick release for stews and chilis, but Breville's [BPR700 manual](https://s.productreview.com.au/products/manuals/316271_56564e59ba9b1.pdf) defaults its Soup, Casserole and Bolognese presets, which list beef chili, to Auto Quick.

The recipe sites that rank for this query go further. [Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/1356457/when-use-quick-release-instant-pot) lists oatmeal, mac and cheese, risotto and quinoa for quick release, and [Spend With Pennies](https://www.spendwithpennies.com/instant-pot-natural-release-vs-quick-release-pressure-cooker/) recommends it for mac and cheese and spaghetti. Both conflict with Instant's no-quick-release list for pasta and starchy foods. My rule: follow your own model's manual and the recipe, and on an Instant pot treat Instant's FAQ warning as the tiebreaker for anything starchy or foamy.

## Timed natural release: the 10-minute middle ground

A timed (or partial) natural release means waiting a set number of minutes on Seal, then switching to Vent for whatever pressure is left. The "10-minute natural release" in many recipes is a recipe-writer convention: [TwoSleevers](https://twosleevers.com/instant-pot-npr-vs-qpr/) says it is her usual default. Instant's FAQ and the Rio Plus Wide manual don't prescribe it.

Some cookers build it in. [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/equipment_reviews/2242-multicookers) notes the Instant Pot Pro can alert you after 5 or 10 minutes of natural pressure release. Breville's Auto Pulse mode releases pressure "in bursts to reduce disturbance to the food inside", per its manual, and the release setting can be changed during the release cycle. When a recipe says "natural release for 10 minutes", use the natural steps above, then the quick-release steps below.

## How long does an Instant Pot natural release take?

No manufacturer source opened for this page gives a time. Instant's FAQ and the Rio Plus Wide manual only say to wait until all pressure is released. [Wirecutter](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/reviews/best-electric-pressure-cooker/) says a natural release "can take as long as 30 minutes". Recipe-site figures are unsourced and inconsistent: Tasting Table says about 30 minutes, and Spend With Pennies says it "can take up to 30 minutes" in one place and 10 to 25 minutes in another.

Wirecutter also says Instant's QuickCool Tray, sold separately and compatible with the Instant Pot Pro, cut up to 15 minutes off the regular release time in its testing, for natural (not quick) release.

On what changes the time, TwoSleevers says it depends mainly on how full the pot is and how much of that is liquid; more liquid means a longer wait.

### Turn Keep Warm off for a faster natural release

Instant's FAQ says: "For a quicker natural release, ensure Keep Warm is turned off." That matters because the Rio Plus Wide manual sets Automatic Keep Warm to on by default for all Pressure Cook programs, and the display counts up after cooking when it's on. Press Keep Warm before or during cooking to turn it off.

## How to quick release an Instant Pot safely

Steps from the Rio Plus Wide manual, with its safety rules:

1. When cooking ends, keep your face, hands and exposed skin away from the steam release valve and float valve.
2. Move the steam release switch to Vent. Don't cover or obstruct the valve or float valve with a cloth or anything else.
3. Wait for all pressure to release, until the steam icon disappears and the display shows OK to Open Lid.
4. Remove the lid without leaning over the cooker.

One caution from Instant's FAQ: don't quick release fatty, oily, thick, high-starch or expanding foods.

If food sputters out of the vent, TwoSleevers advises stopping the quick release and letting pressure fall naturally. That's blogger advice, not a manual instruction, so check your own model's manual. If the float valve stays up after venting, see [why an Instant Pot float valve won't drop](/guides/instant-pot-float-valve/).

### Release controls differ by model

ATK found the [Instant Pot Pro](/reviews/instant-pot-pro-8qt/)'s release switch "keeps your hand away from the hot steam", and a diffuser on the vent makes the steam disperse slightly more gently. [TechGearLab](https://www.techgearlab.com/topics/kitchen/best-pressure-cooker) says the [Duo Plus V4](/reviews/instant-pot-duo-plus-6qt/) lever sits on the other side of the lid from where steam comes out. It also says the Breville's valve opens automatically or by a front button, the only model it tested that doesn't require touching the valve.

[Reviewed](https://reviewed.com/cooking/best-right-now/the-best-electric-multi-cookers-and-instant-pots) says the CHEF iQ beeps before it releases pressure on its own if you choose immediate quick release. ATK says an automatic quick-release button would have been welcome on the Cosori CMC-CO601, but found its manual release "still safe and effective". For the Pro and Breville side by side, see [Instant Pot Pro vs Breville Fast Slow Pro](/vs/instant-pot-pro-8qt-vs-breville-bpr700/); for the Cosori, see [Cosori vs Instant Pot Rio](/vs/cosori-cmc-co601-vs-instant-pot-rio-6qt/).

## When is it safe to open the lid?

Open only when the float valve has dropped and, on models with a display, it shows OK to Open Lid (Rio Plus Wide manual). Instant's FAQ says never to force the lid open: a lid that won't open means the pot is still pressurized. Breville's manual says the same, and to press STEAM RELEASE until all pressure is out.

One exception is an inner pot stuck to the lid after the pressure is gone. The Rio Plus Wide troubleshooting table says cooling can create suction that holds the inner pot to the lid; set Steam Release to Vent to release the vacuum. A lid that opens when it shouldn't is a recall matter. [CPSC 25-247](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2025/SharkNinja-Recalls-1-8-Million-Foodi-Multi-Function-Pressure-Cookers-Due-to-Burn-Hazard-Serious-Burn-Injuries-Reported) covers Ninja Foodi OP300-series cookers whose pressure-cooking lid "can be opened during use". To check your model, see the [pressure cooker recall check](/guides/pressure-cooker-recall-check/) and [are pressure cookers safe](/guides/are-pressure-cookers-safe/).
