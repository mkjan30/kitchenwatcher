A stove shut off device for dementia cuts power or gas to the range when nobody is in the kitchen, when a smoke alarm sounds, or when a timer runs out. Which type fits depends on the person's stage of dementia and whether the stove is gas or electric. The Alzheimer's Association also suggests knob covers, removing the knobs, or turning off the gas when the stove is not in use.

Kitchen Watcher is a research site. Nothing on this page was tested hands-on; device details come from manufacturer pages and are labeled that way ([how we research](/how-we-research/)). This is not medical advice; talk the options through with the person's care team.

## Why the stove is the riskiest appliance for someone with dementia

The US Fire Administration's [Fire Safety for Older Adults](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/downloads/pdf/publications/fa_221.pdf) says cooking fires are the number one cause of home fires. It adds that from age 65, people are twice as likely to be killed or injured by fires, and it names dementia among the conditions that make it harder to react.

NFPA's [Home Cooking Fires report](https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Research/NFPA-Research/US-Fire-Problem/Fire-causes/oscooking.pdf) (Hall and McGree, September 2023) puts the 2017 to 2021 yearly averages at 158,400 home cooking fires, 470 civilian deaths, 4,150 injuries and $1.15 billion in direct property damage. Ranges or cooktops were involved in 53% of those fires and 88% of the deaths. NFPA calls unattended cooking by far the leading factor, and James and Clark's [2025 review of dementia fire safety](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12171083/) lists getting distracted and forgetting that the stove is on among common fire incidents involving people with dementia. For general prevention, see [how to prevent kitchen fires](/guides/how-to-prevent-kitchen-fires/).

## Matching the setup to the stage of dementia

These are options to raise with the care team, not a prescription. The one consistent lesson from the research below is timing: introduce a device while the person can still learn it.

**Early stage: still cooking, needs prompts.** The goal is keeping independence while catching the occasional forgotten pan. A presence shut-off suits this stage because the person does nothing to trigger it. iGuard says its extended cooking timer lets a user keep the stove on for longer dishes. Practice the override together while it can still become routine.

**Middle stage: forgets burners, may not respond to alarms.** Anything that relies on the person noticing or acting becomes weaker. USFA says dementia makes it harder to react to a fire, so a device that cuts power or gas on its own matters more than one that only warns. iGuard says a caregiver can set a scheduled lock, lock the stove remotely and get alerts, including when there is no morning activity. A smoke-alarm listener such as FireAvert adds a backstop, but only once there is already smoke.

**Late stage or no longer safe to cook.** Shut-off devices assume someone is cooking. When that stops being safe, the Alzheimer's Association's steps fit better: knob covers, removing knobs, or turning off the gas when the stove is not in use. Disabling the stove at the breaker or gas valve should be done by a qualified person, with meals arranged another way.

## Knob covers vs shut-off: preventing turn-on vs stopping a burner left on

Knob covers and shut-off devices answer different questions. Covers and removed knobs stop a burner from being turned on. Shut-offs stop a burner that is already on from staying on. The Alzheimer's Association lists both: use appliances with an automatic shut-off feature, and use knob covers, remove knobs or turn off the gas when the stove is not in use.

The gap in each is the other's strength. A knob cover does nothing once someone has got a burner lit. A shut-off does nothing to prevent turn-on unless it has a lock or schedule, which iGuard says its app provides. Someone who still cooks but forgets burners may need a shut-off; someone who should not cook at all may only need the knobs gone.

## Gas vs electric stoves: what changes

Electric is the simpler install. FireAvert describes its electric unit as a plug-and-play install of about five minutes, with a 3-prong version for pre-1996 homes and stoves and a 4-prong version for post-1996. iGuard says it sells separate models for electric stoves and electric cooktops.

Gas means working on the gas line. FireAvert says its gas model adds a solenoid valve on the stove's gas line and needs a wrench; iGuard says its gas version includes a sensor that warns of unburned gas. FireAvert describes gas install as DIY or a handyman job; given the gas leak reported in the research below, we would lean toward a qualified gas installer.

Electric coil ranges that comply with UL 858 have a built-in layer too. Per UL Standards & Engagement, [UL 858](https://ulse.org/insight/standards-and-engagement-standards-matter-household-oven-and-range-standards-helping/) includes a test in which a cast iron pan of cooking oil on a coil element at its maximum setting for 30 minutes must not ignite. That is a range design requirement, not a substitute for supervision or a shut-off.

## What the research says, including where these devices failed

The evidence is thin. James and Clark's 2025 [narrative review in the journal Dementia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12171083/) found only eight eligible peer-reviewed articles on fire-safety guidance, initiatives or interventions for people with dementia living in the community. The reported device problems are specific:

- In one study, all three participants who trialled a stove shut-off withdrew before the end, because of major technical issues (a gas leak and cutting off the mains supply) or problems adjusting to the device changing the cooker's appearance.
- Blown fuses shorted the mains electricity, leaving some people eating cold meals when they could not get the power back on.
- Devices set to switch off after 30 minutes got in the way of meals that needed longer, and switching the timer off early took additional steps.
- Devices introduced too late were seen as "an odd artefact that obstructed cooking," and in one study the changed appearance stopped people using the stove at all.

The review also notes that fire services in England do not routinely record dementia status. Of the sources read for this page, none shows that these devices prevent fire deaths.

A shut-off is meant to lower one risk; it does not guarantee a fire won't start. If one does, USFA's advice stands: slide a lid over the pan and turn off the burner, or get out and call 911. See [how to put out a grease fire](/guides/how-to-put-out-a-grease-fire/).

## Aging in place stove safety checklist

- Working smoke alarms at least 10 feet from the stove, per USFA ([smoke detector near a kitchen](/guides/smoke-detector-near-kitchen/)).
- A pot lid kept by the stove, per USFA.
- An extinguisher suited to the kitchen, and caregivers who know how to use it ([which fire extinguisher for a kitchen](/guides/what-fire-extinguisher-for-kitchen/), [how to use a fire extinguisher](/guides/how-to-use-a-fire-extinguisher/)).
- Check that burners and oven are off after cooking, per USFA.
- Introduce any device early and practice the override with the person there.
- Tell every caregiver how the device resets.

## When is a shut-off device no longer enough?

Watch for repeated shut-off events, burns, or unsafe items put on a burner. The 2025 review describes a plastic kettle on a gas hob as a common accident in practice, one family carers have cited as the reason a relative moved into residential care. When these signs appear, talk with the care team about ending independent stove use.
