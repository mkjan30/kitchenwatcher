Toaster wattage typically runs from 800 to 1,400 watts, per a [Virginia Tech extension appliance table](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/2901/2901-9014/2901-9014.html) developed as part of the NASULGC/DOE Building Science Community of Practice. Your model's own figure is stamped on its rating label, usually on the bottom or back. At the US average electricity price, one toasting cycle works out to roughly a third of a cent to just over a cent, depending on wattage and cycle length.

## How many watts does a toaster use? Sources disagree at the top end

The [Virginia Tech publication](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/2901/2901-9014/2901-9014.html) lists nameplate wattages of 800 to 1,400 W for toasters and 1,225 W for toaster ovens.

Other sources give wider ranges. [ecocostsavings](https://ecocostsavings.com/toaster-wattage/) collected the listed wattage of the 100 most-sold US toasters on Amazon and found 500 to 1,800 W, with an average of 1,096 W and 750 W the most common figure. In the same survey, 4-slice models averaged 1,515.3 W against 880 W for 2-slice models. [Anker SOLIX's blog](https://www.ankersolix.com/blogs/solar/how-many-watts-does-a-toaster-use) says most household toasters fall between 800 and 1,500 W, without citing a source.

So the low end is broadly agreed, but the top end is not. The [2-slice vs 4-slice guide](/guides/2-slice-vs-4-slice-toaster/) covers what the extra slots buy you.

## Where to find your toaster's wattage: rating label or amps x volts

Virginia Tech says the wattage of most appliances is stamped on the bottom or back, or on the nameplate. If only amps are listed, multiply amps by voltage; it notes that most US appliances use 120 V.

Be careful with figures from non-US manuals. A Middle East booklet from Hamilton Beach Brands for the [Proctor Silex 22614-SAU](https://useandcares.hamiltonbeach.com/files/840265801.pdf) rates that toaster at 220-240 V and 750 W, while Hamilton Beach's [US toaster booklet](https://useandcares.hamiltonbeach.com/files/840216701.pdf) refers to a rating label such as 120 V ~ 60 Hz. A wattage taken from a UK, Australian or Middle East listing may not match the US version of the same toaster.

## Toaster electricity cost: the formula and a worked example

Virginia Tech's formula is wattage x hours used / 1,000 = kWh; multiply kWh by your utility's rate to get the cost. For the rate, the [EIA's Electric Power Monthly](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a) gives a US average residential price of 18.16 cents per kWh for August 2026, a preliminary estimate, against 17.57 cents in August 2025.

> **Worked example (our arithmetic, upper bounds)**
>
> - Low case: 800 W (bottom of Virginia Tech's range) x 85 s (ATK's fastest average, the Magimix Vision) = about 0.019 kWh. At 18.16 cents (EIA, August 2026), that is about 0.34 cent per cycle.
> - High case: 1,400 W (top of Virginia Tech's range) x 178 s (ATK's slowest average, the Smeg it lists as a 4-slice) = about 0.069 kWh, or about 1.26 cents per cycle.
> - One cycle a day for a year: about 6.9 kWh ($1.25) in the low case, about 25.3 kWh ($4.59) in the high case.

These pair range ends with times from different toasters, so they bracket the cost rather than describe any one model. They also assume the toaster draws its full rated wattage for the whole cycle. Virginia Tech notes that actual consumption depends on the setting in use. ATK's times are for one slice; filling every slot may change the cycle, and ATK doesn't report that.

## What measured numbers exist, and how thin they are

[ecocostsavings](https://ecocostsavings.com/toaster-wattage/) reports one metered toaster: a 4-slice model rated 2,100 W on a 230 V supply that drew a typical 1,901 W while toasting and peaked at 1,921 W, 91.5% of its label. It also reports energy-monitor readings of 0.05 kWh for 2 slices on medium and 0.07 kWh on the highest setting, and 0.11 and 0.13 kWh for 4 slices. The page doesn't name the meter or the toasters used for the kWh readings.

[Cockeyed](https://cockeyed.com/science/power_use_database/toaster.html) reports one unnamed toaster at 904 W, with no meter or method given. Its figures also don't add up: 87 cents a day would be about $26 over 30 days, not the $16.50 a month it lists.

ecocostsavings has an arithmetic slip too. It says toasting 4 slices continuously on the highest setting would cost 45 cents an hour at 26.71 cents per kWh, but its own 1.8 kWh at that rate works out to about 48 cents.

No independent lab we found publishes per-model toaster energy use. [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/equipment_reviews/1949-long-slot-toasters), [TechGearLab](https://www.techgearlab.com/topics/kitchen/best-toaster) and [Reviewed](https://www.reviewed.com/cooking/best-right-now/the-best-toasters) report toasting results, not wattage measurements. Kitchen Watcher did not meter any toaster for this page.

## Does higher wattage mean faster toast? The evidence is thin

Anker SOLIX's blog says a 1,000 W toaster will typically toast faster than a 700 W one, without test data. The data we have can't confirm or refute that. ATK's averages run from 1 min 25 s for the Magimix Vision to 2 min 58 s for the Smeg 4-slice, but we have no confirmed wattage for those models, so time and wattage can't be matched.

What the formula does show: at the same wattage, a longer cycle uses more kWh. The [toaster settings guide](/guides/toaster-settings-explained/) explains the shade dial.

## Toaster energy use vs your circuit: what the manuals say

Hamilton Beach's [US toaster booklet](https://useandcares.hamiltonbeach.com/files/840216701.pdf) says: "To avoid an electrical circuit overload, do not use another high-wattage appliance on the same circuit with this appliance." It allows an approved extension cord only if its electrical rating is equal to or greater than the appliance's.

Breville's [Australia/New Zealand booklet for the BTA720 and BTA730](https://assets.breville.com/Instruction-Booklets/ANZ/BTA730_IB_A13_WEB.pdf) (220-240 V models) and Breville's [US book for the same models](https://assets.breville.com/BTA720/BTA720_BTA730_USCM_IB_F26_LR.pdf) both recommend plugging the toaster directly into its own outlet on a dedicated circuit, separate from other appliances, because an overloaded circuit may stop it working properly. They say use with a power strip or extension cord is not recommended; if one is used anyway, the cord's rating should be at least the appliance's and it should have a 3-prong grounding plug.

The [US Fire Administration's](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prevent-fires/cooking/) cooking-safety graphic says not to use extension cords for kitchen appliances. We don't give breaker amp limits here, because we didn't open a source for them. For wider fire prevention, see [how to prevent kitchen fires](/guides/how-to-prevent-kitchen-fires/).

## Standby power: unplug when not in use

ecocostsavings says toasters with digital displays or controls draw about 1 W when plugged in but idle, and that about 19% of the 100 most popular US toasters have such controls; it gives no measurement for the 1 W figure. Hamilton Beach's US booklet says to always unplug the toaster when not in use. The safety side of that is covered in [is it safe to leave a toaster plugged in](/guides/is-it-safe-to-leave-a-toaster-plugged-in/).
