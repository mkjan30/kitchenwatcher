SCA certified means a coffee maker passed lab tests against the Specialty Coffee Association's home-brewer standard. For a coffee maker, that means the water stays hot enough through the whole brew, the brew finishes inside a set time window, and the coffee lands in the Golden Cup range for strength and extraction.

Testing is now done by a third-party lab, the European Coffee Brewing Centre (ECBC), and the mark is licensed for a three-year term.

The SCA's published description says nothing about how long the machine lasts or how often owners report failures. Only one secondary source here (Coffee Chronicler) says carafe heat is tested. The badge also isn't permanent, which is why many "certified" lists online include machines the SCA no longer shows.

## What the SCA actually tests: the Golden Cup criteria

The SCA's archived certification page says the requirements are "based on proper water temperature, brewing time, and ability to brew within the SCA Golden Cup recommendations." The full rules sit in a standard called SCA-310, Home Coffee Brewers: Specifications and Test Methods. Per the [SCA standards page](https://sca.coffee/research/coffee-standards), that document is available only to SCA members, and the page publishes no numbers.

So every figure below comes from a secondary source, and they don't all agree. None of them is the official text.

| Criterion | What published sources say | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| Brew temperature | 194–205°F, held for the whole cycle | [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/equipment_reviews/2461-best-coffee-makers-automatic-drip) |
| Brew temperature | 197–205°F (92–96°C) | [CNET](https://www.cnet.com/home/kitchen-and-household/best-coffee-maker/), "according to the Specialty Coffee Association" |
| Contact / brew time | 4 to 8 minutes | CNET; [Coffee Chronicler](https://coffeechronicler.com/best-drip-coffee-makers/); Seattle Coffee Gear; Cosori |
| Strength (TDS) | 1.15–1.35% | Coffee Chronicler; Seattle Coffee Gear |
| Extraction yield | 18–22% | America's Test Kitchen; Coffee Chronicler; Seattle Coffee Gear; [Coffeeble](https://www.coffeeble.com/gear/coffee-makers/scaa-certified/) (CNET calls 18–20% "commonly thought" ideal) |
| Even wetting of the grounds | Required, no number published | Cosori; [Barista Life](https://baristalife.co/blogs/blog/sca-certified-coffee-makers); Coffeeble |
| Coffee-to-water ratio | 55 g per litre, about 1:18 | America's Test Kitchen; Seattle Coffee Gear; Cosori |
| Coffee-to-water ratio | 1:16 to 1:18 | [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/home/coffee-makers/best-coffee-makers) |

These figures come from the sources named in each row. The SCA's full brewer standard is available to members only, so I couldn't check them against the document itself.

Coffee Chronicler adds that serving temperature must hold for at least 30 minutes. None of the other sources here mention that, and the SCA hasn't published it.

## The SCA brew temperature range, and why sources quote different numbers

Published figures for the SCA brew temperature range don't match:

- 194–205°F: America's Test Kitchen
- 197–205°F, or 92–96°C: CNET
- 195–205°F: Coffee Chronicler, Cosori (both also give 90–96°C) and Seattle Coffee Gear
- 196–205°F: Tom's Guide
- 92–96°C (about 198–205°F): Coffeeble

The top end, 205°F, is the same everywhere. The bottom end moves by a few degrees, and none of these sources quotes the SCA document directly; the SCA keeps it members-only. I can't settle the exact figure from the public record, so treat any single number as a summary, not the rule.

The SCA's own research suggests a few degrees matter less than the arguments imply. An article in the SCA's [25 Magazine](https://sca.coffee/sca-news/25/issue-15/how-hot-is-hot-enough-brew-temperature-sensory-profile-and-consumer-acceptance-of-brewed-coffee-5dj5b) describes UC Davis Coffee Center research, run with the SCA's Coffee Science Foundation and underwritten by Breville. The researchers brewed at 87–93°C while holding strength and extraction constant. Flavor changes from temperature alone were small enough to be considered mostly insignificant, and temperature wasn't an important predictor of how much the 118 consumer tasters liked the coffee.

The article's own takeaway: think about temperature mainly through its effect on extraction. That is a reading of one research series, and the same article notes drip brewers that can't reach 92°C fast enough fail certification.

## Who does the testing, and what certification costs

Per the SCA's [current certification page](https://sca.coffee/certified-home-brewer):

- Only equipment fully evaluated at the SCA's third-party testing centre, ECBC, is considered.
- An active SCA Company Membership is required to apply.
- Every submission gets a detailed test report within about two weeks, pass or fail.
- The fee is USD $5,000 a year on a three-year licensing term.

[ECBC](https://ecbc.coffee) describes itself as an independent testing authority established in 1971 by the Norwegian Coffee Association to test brewing equipment to the Gold Cup Standard. Its news list includes a March 2023 press release titled "ECBC – test partner for SCA."

The [archived March 2020 version](https://web.archive.org/web/20200309224550/https://sca.coffee/certified-home-brewer) of the SCA page adds two rules the current page doesn't spell out. Certification lasted three years or until the design of production models changed, whichever came first, after which the model had to be re-tested. Manufacturers sent five units of a production model for testing and again for re-tests. It also said that as of 2014 the SCA had renewed its cooperation with the European Coffee Brewing Center, so ECBC's involvement predates the 2023 partnership.

The archived March 2020 SCA list was longer and looked different. It included 12 Technivorm Moccamaster models (among them the K, KB, KBG, KBGT, KBT and KBTS), the Breville Precision Brewer, Ninja CM401 and CM407, Bonavita BV1900TS, Behmor Brazen Plus 2.0, Cuisinart PurePrecision and OXO On 9-Cup. None of these appear on today's page.

Roundups haven't caught up. America's Test Kitchen (published June 11, 2025) marks four models "SCA Certified: Yes": the Moccamaster KBT, Moccamaster KBGV Select, OXO 8-Cup and Fellow Aiden. Tom's Guide calls the KBGV Select "Specialty Coffee Association-approved," and Coffee Chronicler names the Breville Precision Brewer as certified. Coffeeble's list includes the KBGV Select, xBloom Studio, Ratio Six Series 2 and Behmor Brazen Plus 3.0, none of which are on the live list.

Barista Life is the exception: it notes that Moccamasters aren't on the current page, which it says lists machines certified from 2023 onward, and that earlier certifications aren't automatically shown.

Why the gap? The current page states a three-year licensing period, the archived page added a re-test after any design change, and every entry on today's list is dated 2023 or later. That explains how a model can drop off the list, but I have no source saying why any specific model isn't there now, and absence isn't evidence it failed a test. ## What the SCA badge doesn't tell you

- **How steady the temperature is outside the SCA lab.** [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/equipment_reviews/2461-best-coffee-makers-automatic-drip) tracked water temperature and found the OXO 8-Cup in the 194–205°F band for 54% of the brew cycle and the Fellow Aiden for 57%. The Moccamaster KBT and KBGV Select, neither on the current list, each measured 91%. Among the models ATK marks as certified, results ran from 20% (a Mr. Coffee Perfect Brew Intelligent model) to 94%.
- **How long a full pot takes.** Full-pot cycle time isn't contact time, but it hints at it. [CNET](https://www.cnet.com/home/kitchen-and-household/best-coffee-maker/) timed 8 cups at 4 min 35 s on the OXO 8-Cup and 4 min 41 s on the Breville Luxe. The same test took 10 min 30 s on the uncertified Ninja CE251 and 12 min on the Cuisinart PerfecTemp 14-Cup.
- **Durability.** None of the SCA pages describes a reliability or lifespan test. Owner-reported failures for specific models are covered in our reviews, such as the [OXO Brew 8-Cup review](/reviews/oxo-brew-8-cup/).
- **Your recipe.** Cosori says testing uses the SCA ratio of 55 g per litre. Tom's Guide says the Golden Cup standard works out to about 2 tablespoons of coffee per 5 oz cup and calls the result very full-bodied and not to everyone's taste. A different dose gives a different strength from the one the brewer was certified at.

## Is an SCA certified coffee maker worth it?

Certification is a useful floor. It tells you a third-party lab tested the brewer against the SCA's temperature, time and extraction requirements and it passed.

It isn't a ranking. ATK's measurements show certified machines spent anywhere from 20% to 94% of the brew cycle in range, and CNET's under-$100 pick, the [Ninja CE251](/reviews/ninja-ce251/), isn't on the list. Price, reliability and carafe heat retention come from other evidence, which is what the [best coffee makers](/best/coffee-makers/) hub and [Ninja CE251 vs Cuisinart DCC-3200](/vs/ninja-ce251-vs-cuisinart-dcc-3200/) weigh.

If two machines are otherwise close, the badge is a fair tiebreaker. If you'd pay much more for it alone, the evidence doesn't back that.

## How to check whether a specific model is SCA certified

1. Open the SCA's [certified home brewer page](https://sca.coffee/certified-home-brewer) on the day you buy.
2. Match the exact model name and number, not just the brand.
3. Match the carafe variant. The SCA lists some thermal and glass versions in one entry (GE Café Specialty Drip, Breville Luxe Brewer). The OXO 8-Cup entry shows a listing can differ from an older announcement.
4. Treat box logos and retailer badges with care. The mark is licensed for a three-year term, so packaging can outlast it.
5. If the model isn't listed, ask the manufacturer for its certification year and whether the current production design was re-tested.
