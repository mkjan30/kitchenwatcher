What wattage microwave do I need? For most people, about 900 to 1,000 watts of output power. [America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/equipment-reviews/2537-the-best-microwave-ovens) found that at 900 to 1,000 W "you can simply use full power for the suggested amount of time" on a package. A 700 W oven works but needs extra time, and for high-power ovens ATK says to use less time or a lower power setting.

Read the output figure on the box or rating plate, not the input. Output is the microwave cooking power, and it's the figure ATK compares ovens by. Input is what the oven pulls from the wall, and in all three manuals we read it's the bigger number, which makes it easy to quote by mistake.

## Microwave input vs output watts: which number counts

Output watts are the number that matters when you ask what wattage microwave you need. It's the oven's rated microwave cooking power, and it's the "wattage" USDA FSIS links to cooking speed. The input rating is the electrical power the oven draws from the outlet.

Three manuals list both figures (manufacturer spec):

- The [Toshiba EM131A5C-BS instruction booklet](https://images.homedepot-static.com/catalog/pdfImages/fb/fb6df202-147c-4227-8107-017db04f8ed2.pdf) gives 1,550 W rated input and 1,100 W rated output.
- Breville's BMO650 instruction book gives 1,350 W power input and 900 W power output.
- IKEA's GÅTEBO manual gives 1,350 W rated input and 900 W rated output for the microwave.

This is where listings go wrong. [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/us/best-microwaves,review-6316.html) lists the Toshiba EM131A5C-BS in its spec box as "Power: 1550 watts", while its text says 1,000 W. The manual says 1,100 W output and 1,550 W input, so the spec box matches the input figure and the text matches neither. If a listing's single "power" figure doesn't match the manual's rated output, trust the manual.

### The IEC test number, and why one manual gives two figures

Output ratings come from a standard test, per IKEA's GÅTEBO manual, which footnotes its output figure: "The International Electrotechnical Commission's standardized method for measuring output wattage was used."

The same manual contradicts itself, though. Its spec table says 900 W output, but its cooking text says the oven "is rated 1000 watts by using the IEC Test Procedure." We can't tell from the manual which figure is right. If you own a GÅTEBO, treat it as a 900 W oven and check food early, which is what the manual's text advises anyway.

## 700 vs 1000 watt microwave: what changes

The main difference between a 700 vs 1000 watt microwave is time. [USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/cooking-microwave-ovens) says plainly: "The higher the wattage of a microwave oven, the faster it will cook food."

America's Test Kitchen's lineup ran from 700 to 1,250 W of output. The 700 W Galanz 0.7 Cu Ft Retro "often needed more time to complete tasks," but it "never burned or overcooked food." At the other end, the 1,200 W Panasonic NN-SN67KS was "sometimes a bit too powerful" and overheated and curdled frozen mac and cheese. ATK's conclusion: "You might assume the higher the wattage the better, but this was not necessarily true."

Sources disagree on whether low wattage heats unevenly. [RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/microwave/reviews/best/countertop) says higher output can heat food faster, but it found "no real difference in how evenly microwaves heat your food." [Reviewed's 2019 buying explainer](https://www.reviewed.com/small-appliances/features/buying-microwave-why-never-pay-too-much-money) says the opposite, that lower wattage "can heat your food unevenly." Reviewed's piece is older; we lean toward RTINGS, but neither settles it.

RTINGS also scores power. It gives the Sharp SMC2266KS (1,200 W, manufacturer spec) a power score of 9.9 and the Toshiba EM131A5C-BS (1,100 W output, manufacturer spec) 9.4.

## 900 watt microwave cooking times: how to adjust a package time

For 900 watt microwave cooking times, start from the package time, check early and add time until the food is hot enough. USDA FSIS gives the rule: "Use the minimum cooking time given for high wattage ovens; use the maximum cooking time for slow ovens. The minimum cooking time may need to be reduced for very high wattages."

1. **Find your output wattage** on the rating plate or in the manual (see below).
2. **Pick the package time that fits.** FSIS's time-to-boil scale (below) puts ovens of about 800 W or more in its high-wattage group, so by FSIS's rule a 900 W or 1,100 W oven starts at the minimum time, and a very high-wattage oven may need less. The maximum time is for slow ovens of 300 to 500 W. FSIS also says that if a range is given, start with the fewest minutes and add time to reach a safe internal temperature.
3. **Check early.** IKEA's GÅTEBO manual says to "check food a minute or two before the minimum time and add time accordingly."
4. **If the package names one wattage and one time,** a starting point we worked out (not a rule from any source) is: package time × package watts ÷ your watts. For a 3-minute time written for 1,100 W, a 900 W oven gives about 3 minutes 40 seconds. Treat it as a first guess.
5. **Stir or turn the food halfway.** [Nebraska Extension](https://food.unl.edu/article/microwave-safety/) says stirring, rotating or flipping midway helps eliminate cold spots.
6. **Let it stand.** FSIS and Nebraska Extension both say to allow at least 3 minutes of standing time.
7. **Check with a food thermometer in several places.** FSIS warns that microwaves "can cook unevenly and leave cold spots." Its minimums: leftovers 165°F, poultry 165°F, ground meat 160°F.

### Power levels are a share of the output watts

A power level is a percentage of the oven's output. IKEA's GÅTEBO manual spells it out: P100 is 900 W, P50 is 450 W and P10 is 90 W. Toshiba's EM131A5C-BS manual lists ten power levels, with level 10 at 100%.

FSIS explains that below 100% the magnetron cycles on and off, so 50% means it runs about half the time. The GÅTEBO also drops itself to 80% after 20 minutes at 100% to protect the magnetron, per its manual. How inverter ovens differ is covered in [is an inverter microwave worth it](/guides/is-an-inverter-microwave-worth-it/).

## Don't know your wattage? How to find it

USDA FSIS says to look inside the door, on the serial-number plate on the back of the oven, or in the owner's manual.

If you still can't find it, FSIS describes a "Time-to-Boil" test. Put 1 cup of ice-cold water in a 2-cup glass measure, heat it on high, and watch for up to 4 minutes:

- Boils in under 2 minutes: 1,000 W or more.
- About 2½ minutes: about 800 W or more.
- About 3 minutes: 650 to 700 W or more.
- Over 3 minutes, or not boiling by 4: 300 to 500 W.

That is USDA's rough estimate, not a measurement. Always put the water in: Toshiba's and IKEA's manuals list running the oven with nothing inside as a cause of arcing. If the water barely warms at all, see [microwave not heating](/guides/microwave-not-heating/).

## Is more wattage worth paying for?

Sources don't agree on the minimum.

Tom's Guide says "You should find 800-1200 watts sufficient." Reviewed's 2019 explainer says to buy 1,000 W. [TopTenReviews](https://www.toptenreviews.com/what-microwave-wattage-is-best) quotes a chef who says to aim for at least 800 W, and an appliance-repair shop owner who calls 1,000 W the sweet spot for most households but says smaller or lighter-use households can go with 600 to 700 W. ATK says higher is "not necessarily" better.

Our reading of that evidence, not a test: buy the output wattage that matches the package times you cook from most, which ATK's results put around 900 to 1,000 W. More watts mostly saves minutes, and none of the sources we read show it changing what an oven can cook.
